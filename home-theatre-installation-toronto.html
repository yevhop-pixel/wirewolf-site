What you're paying for

Calibration is the job. The speaker box is just the material.

Anyone can run a length of speaker wire to a subwoofer and call it a home theatre. What separates a system that sounds like the demo room at the shop from one that sounds like a TV with extra boxes bolted around it is calibration — and it's the step most installers skip, because it's slower than screwing brackets to a wall and nobody standing in the room can tell it didn't happen.

Real calibration means levels matched with a meter, not by ear from one spot on the couch. It means distances to each speaker measured to the inch, because the receiver uses that number to time the sound so it arrives together instead of staggered by a few milliseconds you'd swear you can't hear until you can. It means crossover points set to match what your speakers can actually reproduce, so the subwoofer picks up exactly what the mains hand off and nothing gets doubled or dropped. It means room correction is run, measured from more than one seat, and checked against what actually comes out of the speakers — not switched on with the defaults and left.

Skip any of that and you still get sound. You just don't get a home theatre — you get a loud TV with more speakers to dust. Your installer here is a certified AV field engineer and control-system programmer, ex-Bell, with a commercial AV background. Calibration and one-remote programming aren't add-on skills on that résumé — they're the two things it's actually for.

Wiring & speakers

In-wall, in-ceiling or bookshelf — wired clean, not stapled to the baseboard

In-wall and in-ceiling speakers get run the way they should be — fished through the stud and joist bays behind finished drywall, not stapled along the baseboard in a plastic raceway, unless you specifically want that look for a rented unit or an easy future move. Where an existing wall genuinely has no usable path, we'll tell you honestly instead of forcing a route that ends up as a bulge behind the paint.

In-wall and in-ceiling speakers disappear visually and clear the floor, which matters in a room that has to double as a living room the other twenty-three hours a day. The trade-off is a touch less low-end extension than a comparable floor-standing speaker, and more work if a driver ever needs replacing — you're opening a wall, not unplugging a cable. Bookshelf and floor-standing speakers hit harder for their size and swap out in five minutes if you upgrade later, but they're furniture you have to plan the room around. Most installs land on in-wall for the surrounds and rears, where nobody's looking anyway, and stand-mounted or floor-standing up front where output actually matters.

None of this requires new gear. If you already own a receiver with enough clean channels and a matched set of speakers in reasonable shape, we build the calibration around what's already on your shelf — we'd rather spend the visit on setup than sell you equipment you didn't need. And if you're mounting a TV in the same room, TV mounting starts at $129, with in-wall cable concealment for that connection a flat $79 add-on, same visit.

Projector or TV

Projector or an 85-inch TV — the honest answer, before you buy either

Ambient light decides between a projector and a big TV more reliably than any spec sheet does. A projector wants a room where light is controlled — blackout curtains, a finished basement, or a media room nobody expects to use at two in the afternoon with the blinds open. Put a projector in a bright living room and you're fighting a losing battle against your own windows; the image goes flat and washed out no matter how many lumens the box claims.

If the room gets real daylight and blackout isn't happening, an 85-inch TV is the honest recommendation. It holds contrast and colour in a lit room, it doesn't need a screen dropped or a bulb replaced down the road, and it resells better than most projectors when you eventually upgrade.

Screen size matters more than the number printed on the box — a 100-inch projected image on a wall twelve feet from the couch does less for you than a well-chosen 85-inch panel at the right viewing distance. We measure the room, check the light, and tell you which one actually earns its keep before you buy either. Projector and screen installs start at $549, mounted, focused and aligned to the room, not eyeballed from a stepladder.

Home theatre FAQ

Straight answers about home theatre installation in Toronto

What does calibration actually include, and why does it matter?
Speaker levels matched with a meter, not by ear. Distances measured to the inch so the receiver's delay timing lines up. Crossover points set to match what your speakers can actually reproduce, so the subwoofer isn't fighting the mains. Room correction run and checked, not left on factory defaults. Skip this step and you get a system that's loud in the wrong places and clear in none of them — dialogue buried, bass that booms in one seat and vanishes in the next.
Can you use my existing speakers and receiver?
Usually, yes. If your receiver has enough clean channels and your speakers are a matched set in reasonable shape, we build the calibration around what you own — no reason to sell you gear you don't need. We'll tell you plainly if something is holding the system back, like a receiver with no room-correction hardware or speakers that don't match front to back, but that's a call you make, not one we make for you.
In-wall speakers or bookshelf — which is better?
Depends what you're optimizing for. In-wall and in-ceiling speakers disappear visually and free up floor space, but they lose a bit of low-end extension and are more work to service if a driver ever fails. Bookshelf and floor-standing speakers generally hit harder and are a five-minute swap if you upgrade later, but they're furniture you have to live with. Most living rooms end up in-wall for the surrounds and rears, and bookshelf or floor-standing up front where the output matters most.
Should I get a projector or an 85-inch TV?
Ambient light decides this more than the spec sheet does. A room with daylight coming in needs blackout curtains or blinds before a projector makes sense — otherwise the image washes out. A dedicated dark room or a finished basement is a projector's home turf. If the room gets used in daylight and you're not blacking it out, an 85-inch TV holds up better and keeps more resale value. We'll tell you which one your room actually supports before you buy either.
How long does a 5.1 install take?
A standard 5.1 install in a room with accessible wiring runs — surrounds on stands or an existing conduit — is usually a single visit, wired, mounted and calibrated the same day. Fishing wire through finished walls and ceilings, or a dedicated cinema room build, takes longer and gets a specific timeline in your written quote before we start.
Can the wires be hidden in a finished room, not just new construction?
Yes — that's most of the job, not a special request. Speaker wire gets fished through finished walls and ceilings using the existing stud and joist bays wherever a route exists, patched where we have to open drywall. In-wall cable concealment for the TV connection itself is a flat $79 add-on. If a wall genuinely has no path, we'll say so and give you the honest alternative, not a wire stapled along the baseboard.
