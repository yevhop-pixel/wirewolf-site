Condos & high-rises

The honest answer about a balcony Starlink install

Most condo buildings already have bulk internet or building-wide fibre, and for a lot of units that's genuinely fine — we're not going to talk you into a $449 install you don't need. The calls we get are usually one of two things: a unit where the building's internet has been unreliable for months and management won't fix it, or someone who wants Starlink specifically as a backup line for a home office, so a building outage doesn't cost them a workday.

The thing that actually decides whether it's possible is the sky view, not the floor. Starlink needs a genuine, unobstructed slice of open sky — for most Toronto exposures that means a real northern view with nothing in the way: no neighbouring tower, no roof overhang, no balcony glass or privacy screen cutting the angle down to nothing. We check this first, from photos or a quick visit, before anyone books an install date. A high floor with an open northern exposure is usually straightforward. A low floor boxed in by taller buildings on three sides usually isn't, and we'll say so on the phone.

Mounting to a balcony railing means touching a shared exterior surface, so condo board approval comes first. We do not currently carry commercial general liability insurance and cannot provide a certificate of insurance. If your building requires one, we cannot accept that work at present. And if your specific unit's exposure genuinely will not work, we'll tell you that outright instead of mounting a dish that never holds a signal. We'd rather lose the job than leave you with an expensive paperweight bolted to your railing.

Townhouses & detached

Roof and gable mounts on tight city lots

Etobicoke, North York and Scarborough are full of exactly the lot sizes that make a satellite install more of a puzzle than a suburban acreage ever is: narrow setbacks, mature trees the city won't let you touch, a neighbour's addition sitting closer to your roofline than the last provider's map accounted for. A standard roof or gable mount is still the right answer for most of these — $449 flat — but it only works if the survey confirms the sky view is actually clear from that spot, not just clear enough on paper.

Townhouses add their own wrinkle: shared walls mean the cable entry point has to be planned around what's actually your unit and what isn't, and a roofline that a neighbour two doors down already has an antenna bolted to isn't necessarily the same angle you'll get. When tree canopy or a blocked roofline rules out the standard mount, a pole or non-penetrating mount — $549 — gets the dish clear without cutting into a roof you'd rather leave alone.

Either way, the cable gets dressed along brick, siding or stucco and sealed properly at the entry point — not stapled along a fence line or left as a silicone blob through the wall.
